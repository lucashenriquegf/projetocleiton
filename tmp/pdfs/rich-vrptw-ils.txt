ABSTRACT
The vehicle routing problem with multiple real constraints is one of the leading logistics challenges for 
technology-based companies. Despite being a classic and well-studied real-world routing problem, only a few 
companies have implemented algorithms to solve it entirely and automatically. Based on a real case study of a 
technology-based company, this paper proposes a solution to an asymmetric close-open mixed vehicle routing 
problem that considers capacity constraints, heterogeneous fleet, time windows, and computational and oper -
ational implementation constraints. A hybrid algorithm is proposed, which combines an iterative local search 
strategy with a set-partitioning problem strategy, utilizing a computing architecture that meets all the constraints 
to solve the considered problem. Benchmarking was conducted using classic instances from the literature and 
actual instances from the company to demonstrate the performance of the proposed methodology. The solutions 
for the company ́ s instances have an average gap of 9% compared with the upper bound obtained via the related 
knapsack problem. In contrast, the results for classical instances have an average gap of 8% compared with the 
best-found solution in the literature. Moreover, the algorithm and the architecture created are already used in the 
company ’ s day-to-day operations, validating the efficiency and effectiveness of the proposed work.
1. Introduction
In the rapidly evolving landscape of modern logistics, the vehicle 
routing problem (VRP) remains a critical lever for cost optimization. 
Transportation costs account for more than 50 % of total logistics costs 
in regions such as Latin America (12 – 14 % of GDP) and developed 
economies (7 – 8 % of GDP) ( Dan, 2021; Departamento Nacional de 
Planeaci ´on, 2021 ). While traditional VRP formulations focus on 
simplified scenarios, industrial applications demand solutions that 
consider asymmetric close-open mixed routes, heterogeneous fleets, 
capacity limits, time windows, and computational feasibility. These 
solutions are real challenges where academic advancements often 
diverge from operational realities. This gap persists despite decades of 
research, leaving many technology-driven firms reliant on fragmented, 
manual planning or generic SaaS tools that sacrifice quality for speed. 
Drawing from a real-world case study, this work bridges theory and 
practice by proposing a hybrid metaheuristic — combining iterative local 
search (ILS) with set-partitioning optimization — embedded within a 
scalable computational architecture. Our approach addresses multifac -
eted routing constraints and integrates critical implementation factors 
such as horizontal scalability and software compatibility within a deci -
sion support system validated in daily operations. Benchmarking against 
classical instances and company-specific data reveals competitive gaps 
of 6 – 8 % versus theoretical bounds. Moreover, the deployed system now 
drives measurable cost reductions, demonstrating that rigorous algo -
rithms can be effectively transitioned into industrial tools without 
compromising their real-world applicability.
The VRP, in its canonical form, seeks to minimize transportation 
costs by designing routes for a given fleet of vehicles serving 
geographically dispersed customers. However, real-world logistics 
operations — particularly in technology-centric enterprises — require ex -
tensions based on combinations of different problems, such as capaci -
tated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP) ( Bernal et al., 2018; Linfati and 
Escobar, 2018 ), Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW)

A persistent chasm exists between algorithmic innovation and in -

faster — often neglect implementation barriers such as legacy system

lem of a technology company, considering the asymmetric costs of open-

refines solutions through perturbation and local search, with a set- 
partitioning model to allocate routes across a mixed fleet optimally. 
The algorithm is encapsulated within a microservice architecture 
designed for low-latency responses and seamless integration with the 
company ’ s existing ERP systems.
The primary contributions of this work are the following: 
• ILS ’ s explorative strength is combined with set-partitioning ’ s preci -
sion to handle time windows, heterogeneous fleets, and asymmetric 
travel costs.
• Embeds computational constraints — including Python-to-Go inter -
operability, containerized deployment, and horizontal scaling — into 
the algorithmic architecture, ensuring real-world usability.
• Benchmarks against classical VRPTW instances (e.g., Solomon 
datasets) and proprietary company data demonstrate an 8 % average 
gap compared to literature benchmarks and a 6 % gap against a 
theoretical upper bound derived from a related knapsack 
formulation.
• Publishes anonymized real-world instances to foster academic- 
industry collaboration on constrained routing problems.
• The multi-pass approach, which iteratively refines the current solu -
tion using the SP scheme, requires more computation time but yields 
a better solution than a single-pass scheme. Indeed, the muti-pass 
approach can be more easily adapted to RVRP domains and con -
straints. Each pass can be tailored to address a specific aspect of the 
problem, making it easier to adjust the heuristic as circumstances 
change.
This paper also quantifies the “ adaptation cost ” of aligning academic 
algorithms with operational realities — a metric seldom explored in the 
literature. For example, while the proposed algorithm ’ s 6 – 8 % perfor -
mance gap may appear modest, it represents a 23 % cost reduction 
compared with the company ’ s prior manual planning processes, vali -
dating the trade-off between optimality and practicality.

2. Literature review
The vehicle routing problem (VRP) is a fundamental NP-hard prob -
lem in combinatorial optimization. Over the past few decades, numerous 
variants of VRP have been defined, incorporating constraints such as 
time windows, heterogeneous fleets, and multiple depots. Comprehen -
sive surveys and taxonomies of VRP variants are available in the liter -
ature ( Braekers et al., 2016; Konstantakopoulos et al., 2022; Elshaer and 
Awad, 2020 ). In practice, VRPs with realistic sizes are solved predomi -
nantly via heuristic and metaheuristic methods, as exact algorithms 
struggle with instances beyond relatively small sizes. Indeed, exact 
methods are not able to solve VRP instances with more than approxi -
mately 150 customers (see Subramanian et al. (2013) ). However, this 
barrier is significantly pushed. Recently proposed branch-cut-and-price 
algorithms solved much larger instances to prove optimality, with up to 
approximately 200 – 360 customers ( Subramanian et al., 2013; Penna 
et al., 2019; Pecin et al., 2017; Costa et al., 2019 ). However, their 
implementation for real-world practical problems is challenging, which 
hinders the efficient solution of VRP problems ( Penna et al., 2019; Rojas- 
Saavedra et al., 2023; Osorio-Mora et al., 2023 ).
For example, Pecin et al. (2017) developed an improved branch-cut- 
and-price (BCP) algorithm that optimally solved all classical CVRP 
benchmark instances (up to 199 customers) and even certain larger in -
stances with up to 360 customers that were previously considered 
intractable by exact methods. Similarly, a recent survey by Costa et al. 
(2019) noted that state-of-the-art exact methods attained optimal solu -
tions for CVRP instances with over 300 customers and VRP with time 
window (VRPTW) instances with approximately 200 customers. These 
breakthroughs overturn the earlier perception ( Subramanian et al., 
2013 ). The VRP benchmark repository consolidates many of these 
optimal solutions and instances ( CVRPLIB, “ Capacitated Vehicle Rout -
ing Problem Library. ” Accessed: Mar. 24, 2025 ). Despite this progress, 
exact approaches for very large or more complex VRP variants remain 
limited by exponential growth in complexity, underscoring the 
continued importance of heuristic and hybrid methods for rich VRPs.
RVRPs refer to vehicle routing problems that combine multiple 
realistic attributes or constraints within a single problem formulation 
( Penna et al., 2019 ). An RVRP integrates several features simulta -
neously, such as a heterogeneous fleet, time windows, and pickup- 
delivery or other constraints, within one problem. The RVRPs are 
more closely modeled in real-world logistics but are markedly more 
complex due to the combination of constraints. RVRPs have been a focus 
of active research in recent years. The literature evolved a taxonomy for 
VRPs with multiple attributes, sometimes even defining a “ rich ” VRP. 
The reader is referred to detailed reviews by ( Caceres-Cruz et al., 2014; 
Schmid et al., 2013; Lahyani et al., 2015 ) for overviews of various rich 
VRP formulations and early solution approaches. Broadly, these surveys 
highlight that earlier research often addressed each new variant in 
isolation, for example, by developing specialized algorithms for VRP 
with backhauls, split deliveries, or site-dependent constraints, leading to 
a proliferation of variant-specific methods.
In the context of RVRPs, significant advancements in optimization 
methodologies have been proposed to address the complex challenges of 
urban logistics. Mathematical formulations for rich vehicle routing 
problems have been developed by ( Rieck and Zimmermann, 2010; 
Masmoudi et al., 2013; Souza Neto and Pureza, 2016; Ambrosino and 
Cerrone, 2022 ). Mixed-integer linear programming for a rich vehicle 
routing problem (RVRP) considering loading and unloading constraints 
was studied by ( Rieck and Zimmermann, 2010 ). Masmoudi et al. (2013)
propose a mathematical model for an RVRP in an e-commerce company, 
considering various aspects. Souza Neto and Pureza (2016) propose a 
mathematical formulation that represents the delivery operations of 
large volumes of products in dense urban areas. A mixed-integer pro -
gramming model and an experimental algorithm are presented for 
validating an RVRP in a city logistic context ( Ambrosino and Cerrone, 
2022 ). The authors consider a problem faced by a logistics company that

for solving RVRP, such as a fix-and-optimize matheuristic ( Marques 
et al., 2020 ) and a biased-randomized heuristic ( Tordecilla et al., 2021 ),

The main modules used for the proposed approach have been studied 
previously. The optimal splitting procedure has been considered for a 
variant of the VRP by ( Prins, 2004 ). This method was studied early by 
( Beasley, 1983 ). In this step, the first phase computes a giant TSP tour 
for all customers by relaxing the vehicle capacity and maximum tour 
length. The splitting procedure is applied to an auxiliary graph. On the 
other hand, the implementation of building a giant traveling salesman 
problem (TSP) tour has been considered for variants of the vehicle 
routing problem, including the nearest neighbor heuristic ( Escobar 
et al., 2013; Escobar et al., 2014; Escobar et al., 2014 ). Route-first 
Cluster-second methods construct a comprehensive tour that covers all 
customers and then split it into feasible trips. A review of works that 
consider this method for various VRP problems has been published by 
( Prins et al., 2014 ). The Set Partitioning (SP) approach has been suc -
cessfully applied by an exact method available for the CVRP ( Baldacci 
et al., 2008 ), as developed by Fukasawa et al. ( Fukasawa et al., 2006 ). 
This method combines a branch-and-cut with the Set Partitioning (SP) 
problem. The columns of the SP correspond to the set of q-routes that 
contain the set of valid CVRP routes. Since the resulting formulation has 
an exponential number of both columns and rows, this leads to column 
and cut generation for computing the proposed approach. Additionally, 
( Bianco et al., 1994 ) addresses the Multiple Depot Vehicle and Sched -
uling Problem (MD-VSP), formulating it as a Set Partitioning problem 
with side constraints (SP).

3.2. Proposed approach
The problem described in Section 3.1 corresponds to an open-close 
vehicle routing problem with a heterogeneous fleet and time windows 
with some company-specific constraints (real rich routing problem with 
time windows   RVRPTW). Moreover, the literature review revealed -
that the correct management of the heterogeneous fleet is a critical 
factor for the RVRPTW. Consequently, a simplified and adaptive version 
of the ILS-RVND-SP algorithm proposed by ( Subramanian et al., 2013 ) 
was presented to solve the RVRPTW based on the excellent results ob -
tained for versions of the VRP considering heterogeneous fleets. In 
particular, the iterative local search (ILS) and set-partitioning (SP) 
procedures have been adapted for the RVRPTW. Unlike in ( Subramanian 
et al., 2013 ), we consider an extended version of the ILS at three 
different execution times, as dictated by the problem ’ s constraints. 
Similarly, the SP is applied only once in the proposed algorithm. Fig. 1
illustrates the proposed algorithm.
Three main steps are identified in the proposed algorithm. In the first 
step, the algorithm decomposes the RVRPTW into N subproblems that 
are less challenging to solve. The second step seeks to solve each sub -
problem created in the first step. Finally, the third step collects the 
routes from each subproblem to present them to the decision maker.
The first step called the parallel problem generator, divides the initial 
problem into multiple subproblems using an Iterated Local Search (ILS) 
metaheuristic. At this stage, the original Rich Vehicle Routing Problem

(RVRP) is simplified into a Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem 
(CVRP), temporarily excluding the full complexity of real-world con -
straints. Specifically, the vehicle capacity refers to the processing ca -
pacity of the subsequent microservice in terms of the number of distinct 
requests. Notably, this microservice is created due to technological 
constraints associated with horizontal scaling. Given the NP-hard nature 
of vehicle routing problems, solving large-scale instances directly is 
computationally challenging. Consequently, decomposing the problem 
into smaller, manageable subproblems — each defined by the routes 
obtained from the simplified CVRP — allows for parallel processing. 
These subproblems are iteratively refined by incrementally reintroduc -
ing the original constraints, thus balancing computational feasibility 
and solution quality.
The second step involves solving each generated subproblem indi -
vidually to apply a two-stage methodology. The first stage, namely the 
logistic routing planner, comprises two algorithms: the ILS and the SP. 
The ILS is used to solve a simplified version of each subproblem, which 
assumes an unlimited availability of vehicles of each type, thereby 
addressing the fleet size and mixing aspects of the vehicle routing 
problem with time windows (FSMVRPTW). The SP receives the solutions 
proposed by the ILS as input. In particular, the ILS result is a superior 
quality solution that serves as a set of feasible routes (or columns) for the 
SP. In the SP, the vehicle availability constraint is reestablished, thus 
allowing the selection of a feasible solution composed of the best routes 
of the set. In this way, the solved problem takes into account the con -
straints of the RVRPTW. Finally, the second stage is executed, consisting 
of a microservice called Route Refiner, which receives each selected 
route by the SP to apply a final improvement process. For that purpose, 
the SPTW associated with each route created by the SP is solved through 
the integer linear programming (ILP) algorithm. The final step involves 
collecting all the routes generated by the solutions to the different 
subproblems, consolidating them, and presenting the results in a 
decision-maker-friendly information system.
The proposed approach considers operational constraints, which are 
classified into two (2): hard and soft constraints. The hard constraints 
cannot be violated under any circumstances, whereas the soft con -
straints affect the objective function; thus, violating them incurs an 
additional cost. Since the objective function aims to minimize costs, it 
attempts to avoid violating soft constraints unless necessary. Whether a

3.2.1. Iterative local search (ILS) metaheuristic
As described above, the ILS algorithm solves either the associated 
FSMVRPTW or the TSPTW in the route improvement stage of the pro -
posed algorithm. Hence, the most important details of the algorithm ’ s 
design are presented below.
The proposed ILS metaheuristic applies a local search procedure 
considering the inherent requirements of the problem (e.g., the capac -
itated vehicle routing problem (CVRP), the fleet size and mixing vehicle 
routing problem with time windows (FSMVRPTW), or the traveling 
salesman problem with time windows (TSPTW)) to an initial solution 
generated via a construction method depending on the problem to be 
solved. The initial solution is generated via a splitting procedure based 
on the idea proposed by ( Prins, 2004 ), which typically provides high- 
quality, feasible solutions within a short computational time. The 
effectiveness of this initial approach arises from the strategy of grouping 
customers into feasible routes.
Initially, all customers are considered collectively, and a giant trav -
eling salesman problem tour is constructed via the nearest neighborhood 
heuristic ( Escobar et al., 2013; Escobar et al., 2014; Escobar et al., 
2014 ). This TSP solution determines the permutation used by the split 
procedure. An auxiliary multigraph ( Rojas-Saavedra et al., 2023 ) is 
constructed through this sequence, where each arc represents a feasible 
route. All problem-specific constraints are validated during the creation 
of these arcs, ensuring the feasibility of the solution. The resulting 
structure is a directed acyclic graph (DAG), on which a shortest path 
problem must be solved to identify the subset of arcs (routes) of mini -
mum cost, thus yielding a feasible initial solution.
A critical feature of this stage is the handling of the heterogeneous 
fleet. The auxiliary graph is constructed as a multigraph. For any po -
tential route segment (an arc connecting two nodes in the graph), the 
algorithm evaluates its feasibility and cost for every available vehicle 
type (it is important to remark that this cost is in a feedback loop from 
the Set Partitioning phase through the slack variables). This means that 
if a segment can be feasibly served by, for instance, both a small truck 
and a large truck, two parallel arcs are created between the nodes, one 
for each vehicle type, with their respective costs. This multigraph 
structure is fundamental to generating a diverse set of routing options.
Encoding the problem as a permutation order leverages the split 
procedure, delegating constraint validation and cost evaluation to the 
multigraph construction phase. This strategy allows the optimization 
search process to focus solely on identifying the optimal permutation. 
Consequently, it simplifies the definition of general local search opera -
tors (such as swap, insertion, or 2-opt) without requiring extensive 
customization specific to the particularities or dimensions of the 
problem.
This representation encodes the customers in a permutation, namely, 
the Big Route , which goes through all the customers. To decode the 
routes, it is necessary to create an auxiliary graph and solve the Shortest 
Path Problem (SPP). The SPP solution arcs contain the information 
related to each feasible route of the problem being solved. In the ILS 
iterative phase, we use random perturbations that depend on a depth 
parameter d ; they randomly choose and reorganize d nodes of the 
complete route ( Big Route ). For other approaches, extensive computa -
tional tests have been performed to find a suitable parameter value.
The local search is performed via 2-opt. After several computational 
experiments considering different local search operators ( Jiang et al., 
2014; Udin et al., 2017 ) for the considered problem, the best-obtained 
results are obtained via the 2-opt move. In addition, we note that deep 
reinforcement learning methods for vehicle routing problems have 
achieved near-optimum results via node swaps and 2-opt moves ( Wu 
et al., 2019 ). However, the architecture of the VRP is defined by the 
number of possible moves, making it less favorable to expand to the 
general k -opt, leading to lower optimality gaps ( Helsgaun, 2017 ). 
Therefore, we fix this operator for the local search process. Importantly, 
the shortest path faster algorithm (SPFA) is applied to the auxiliary 
graph to obtain the value of the objective function. The SPFA algorithm 
is an improvement over the Bellman – Ford algorithm, which, in this 
specific case, has complexity O ( a ) since it is a directed graph, where a is 
the number of arcs in the graph ( Moore, 1959 ).

auxiliary graph of possible feasible routes, given the sequence and the

routes found in the optimization process (line 12). Restricting both al -

time at the expense of reducing the guarantee that the final solution is a 
local optimum.

3.2.3. Reduced set of feasible routes
During the execution of the ILS algorithm in the logistic routing 
planner microservice, numerous feasible routes are generated due to 
local searches and disturbances. For instances of 120 orders, approxi -
mately 140,000 feasible routes are generated. This set then enters the SP 
process to select those routes (columns) that minimize the total costs. 
The optimization process for the complete set of routes yields a 
computational problem, as creating the linear model requires a signifi -
cant amount of RAM. In addition, the computing time increases because 
the stop criterion of the primal simplex is optimal, and it is necessary to 
perform a greater number of iterations.
The Algorithm 3 procedure was included to reduce the number of 
routes, eliminate redundant routes, and select the percentile p of the 
most promising routes based on an evaluation criterion of the cost per 
transported kilogram. The complete graph of the last iteration of the ILS 
is maintained to avoid cases of infeasibility, which contains the indi -
vidual routes for each request. This algorithm is also used to extract the 
information related to the routes from the pool of arcs. For this reason, 
the algorithm returns a pool of routes, which will feed the SP algorithm. 
Starting from the arc pool (line 1), the route information for each arc is 
extracted (line 2) and added to the routing pool (line 3). Each route into 
the pool is subsequently assessed (line 4). First, the redundancy of each 
route is evaluated to determine if other routes in the pool serve the same 
orders at a lower cost (line 5). Furthermore, it is evaluated if each route 
does not exceed the cost evaluation criteria (line 6). The first cycle, 
which creates the pool of routes (lines 1 – 3), is used for this purpose. The 
cost threshold value a route must exceed to belong to the stipulated p 
percentile is precalculated. Any route that does not pass the cost filter is 
eliminated from the routing pool (lines 6 or 8, respectively). The above 
procedure is used to obtain a reduced pool of routes, thereby alleviating 
the load on the SP algorithm.

3.3. Provision data and system architecture
The obtained data related with the real case and the system archi -
tecture are described as following.
3.3.1. Transportation times matrix
The transit time matrix (TM) is the most critical resource for the 
vehicle routing algorithm. It is the mathematical representation of the 
geolocation of delivery points, from which the proximity between 
points, delivery orders, local searches, disturbances, and the initializa -
tion of solutions are established. This resource in VRP test instances is 
usually a parameter and is calculated via 2D Euclidean distances. 
However, this presents a challenge in the application, as its calculation is 
part of the algorithm ’ s execution time, and factors such as transport 
routes, traffic, and altimetry must be considered.
A third-party service is queried via the API to obtain each value of the 
transport time matrix. We have used HereMaps ® Matrix Routing API v8 
( HereMaps, 2022 ), although GoogleMaps ® , OSMR ® , MicrosoftMaps ® , 
and TimeMatrixAI ® , among others, could be used. Here, Maps ® was 
used to differentiate truck and car transport times, which is necessary for 
the developed service. On the other hand, a cost between 0.5 and 5.0 
USD is usually incurred for every 1,000 transactions to calculate the 
distance matrix. The size of the distance matrix is n
2
, where n is the 
number of locations. It is very costly to calculate the TM each time since 
many times the customers are repeated; therefore, the transport values 
have already been calculated. An exact and complete distance matrix 
between all the pairs of customers would also be very costly since 
neighboring customers would yield similar results that could be grouped 
in single data. Similarly, customers from different cities or countries 
should never be included on the same route; therefore, calculating the 
transportation time between them is unnecessary.
The Uber ® H3 library ( Uber, H3: Uber ’ s Hexagonal Hierarchical 
Spatial Index, 27 06, 2018 ) has been used, which groups geographical 
coordinates of latitude and longitude in hexagons of different resolu -
tions to solve the above problems. This fact allows the TM not to be 
between customers but between H3 hexagons (H3 cells). This situation 
reduces the TM size and the associated costs of this resource. Finally, the 
calculation of transportation time between an origin cell and a desti -
nation cell is stored in an independent database, organized by country 
and city, since these times do not vary significantly over time. Fig. 3
illustrates the implementation using hexagons.
3.3.2. Geographical zone constraints
For the considered problem, some geographical areas do not permit 
the entry and exit of certain types of vehicles, typically due to road 
resistance, low-height bridges, or even tourist reasons. This constraint 
has been implemented by creating logistics zones by city, allowing each 
order to have an associated logistics zone, address, and corresponding 
H3 cell. Each type of vehicle defines which logistics zones it can enter 
and which it cannot. Finally, this constraint is strictly validated in the 
ILS procedure of the logistic routing planning platform.

• Efficient routes for the total time.
• Zoning Routes. We prefer to have routes grouped in the same logis -

• Vehicles with a high occupancy rate.

4.3. VRPTW instances
The algorithm ’ s performance is evaluated in the well-known set of 
instances of Solomon (1987) (Cited in ( Dantzig et al., 1954 ) for the 
VRPTW. These instances were chosen because they represent the 
extreme case in which the proposed algorithm could have the worst 
performance, as they involve a cluster-based routing problem with hard 
time windows. This situation hinders the performance of algorithms that 
prioritize routing first and grouping later. The described instances have 
exactly 100 nodes, an average gap of 21.7 %, and an average execution 
time of 57.28 s for the complete set. Despite the algorithm ’ s limitations, 
competitive results were achieved in terms of execution time.
Fig. 8 shows the sorted optimality gaps, which are colored and 
divided. Note that the green horizontal line highlights the average 
optimality gap for the complete set, illustrating the results obtained for 
the VRPTW instances. Table 7 shows a statistical summary of the char -
acteristics of each group. The columns are the same as those in Table 6 , 
except that the number of vehicles is not considered.
Additionally, the instances that relaxed the time band constraints 
increased the range in which orders could be delivered, and the Gap 
decreased significantly, which again confirms the performance of the 
proposed algorithm in real cases. It would also have good performance 
because, in those cases, the slots are usually standardized and allow for 
much maneuvering, as the time between the start and end of the slot is 
long, enabling the meeting of demand from several customers.

5. Concluding remarks and future work
This paper proposes a hybrid algorithm to solve an RVRP, consid -
ering an asymmetric close-open vehicle routing problem that accounts 
for capacity constraints, heterogeneous fleets, time windows, and 
computational and operational implementation constraints. A combined 
iterative local search and set-partitioning problem strategy is proposed, 
along with a computing architecture that meets all the constraints. The 
problem is considered an RVRPTW and has been generated from the 
reality of a Colombian technology company. Technological and opera -
tional limitations have been taken into account, resulting in high-quality 
solutions. Just as an algorithmic strategy is essential, third-party archi -
tecture and tools are also essential. The obtained results suggest that the 
academic solutions for the last-mile distribution problems yield excel -
lent results and work very well for test instances. However, it is chal -
lenging to implement more robust versions in this context, given the 
computational limitations and the cognitive gap that still exists.
The proposed approach yields good solutions within a short 
computing time. The results for benchmarking instances for VRP vari -
ants are competitive. The evaluated instances are the most challenging 
for the algorithm, as the time windows are tight and do not allow for 
significant improvement in the local search processes. In addition, the 
proposed ILS-SP has a contrary philosophy: it routes first and then is 
grouped. The strategy used to solve the problem involves breaking it 
down into more manageable sub-problems and employing various 
methodologies that enable efficient problem-solving. Although this 
strategy may affect the quality of the obtained solution, it is intuitive and 
computationally compatible with the current requirements of the 
Colombian company.
For future work, three-dimensional packing constraints ( Rojas-Saa -
vedra et al., 2023; Rodríguez et al., 2022 ) should be considered, as the 
company often reprocesses due to routes that cannot be reached 
correctly during vehicle loading and must be redesigned. Additionally, 
the company already has information on the items ’ dimensions, orien -
tations, and load supports for transportation.
