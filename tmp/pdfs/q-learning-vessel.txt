ABSTRACT
Traditional route recommendation systems optimize navigation paths using environmental var -
iables such as weather and sea conditions, but often fail to account for real-world factors 
encountered by mariners. To address this gap, this study proposes a knowledge transfer Q- 
learning (KT-QL) algorithm, a reinforcement learning method built upon the Q-learning frame -
work. The proposed KT-QL algorithm integrates expert trajectory knowledge derived from 
Automatic Identification System data into the Q-learning process, enabling the agent to combine 
trial-and-error exploration with data-driven guidance. Experimental results show that KT-QL 
reduces Hausdorff distances by approximately 39 % compared with conventional reinforce -
ment learning and traditional search methods, and enhances fuel consumption prediction accu -
racy by approximately 2 %. These findings highlight the potential of KT-QL to enhance maritime 
operational efficiency, safety, and environmental sustainability.
1. Introduction
Maritime transportation is pivotal to global trade, making the selection of efficient vessel routes a critical task. Optimizing these 
routes can significantly enhance economic, environmental, and safety outcomes by reducing fuel consumption, shortening voyage 
times, and lowering the risk of maritime accidents ( Lin et al., 2013 ). The International Maritime Organization (IMO) estimates that 
voyage and speed optimization can reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by 1 – 10 % and up to 75 %, respectively ( IMO, 2025 ).
As global demand for decarbonization and environmentally friendly operations gains momentum, the need for such operational 
efficiencies has become even more urgent. In response, major stakeholders, including the IMO and the European Union, have 
implemented stringent regulations to reduce carbon emissions from the shipping industry. Nevertheless, uncertainties surrounding 
large-scale technological investments and guaranteed reduction outcomes have led many shipping companies to prioritize operational 
measures that yield immediate results. Among these, optimized vessel route planning is a widely adopted method because it can 
quickly decrease fuel use and ensure regulatory compliance. In practice, route planning must also account for real-world navigational 
factors such as traffic corridors and stable routes frequently used by other vessels ( Sharif et al., 2024 ).
Although various commercial machine-learning (ML) systems have emerged, proposing efficient routes by incorporating envi -
ronmental variables such as weather, waves, and currents ( Sharif et al., 2024 ). Despite their utility, these approaches often fail to

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising approach for vessel trajectory planning because it can learn navigation 
policies through trial-and-error interactions with the environment. Nevertheless, its direct application in the maritime domain remains 
challenging. Automatic Identification System (AIS) data are often sparse and unevenly distributed; reward design and hyperparameter 
choices strongly affect learning stability; and agents may generate impractical detours when prior navigational knowledge is absent. 
These limitations highlight the need for methods that retain the adaptability of RL while embedding expert-derived priors to ensure 
both convergence stability and operational realism.
To overcome these limitations, this study proposes a knowledge transfer Q-learning (KT-QL) approach that combines the strengths 
of experience-based imitation learning (behavioral cloning, BC) and trial-and-error-based Q-learning, which is one of the most widely 
used RL methods. The KT-QL algorithm uses expert vessel trajectories from AIS data to initialize the agent’s policy, leveraging BC’s 
rapid convergence. Simultaneously, it enhances the generalization capabilities of Q-learning by enabling autonomous exploration of 
the environment. This hybrid structure is designed to mitigate the known limitations of both methods: the compounding errors and 
generalization limits of BC, and the slow, unstable convergence of conventional Q-learning. By balancing exploration and exploitation, 
the proposed algorithm can derive realistic vessel routes that enhance operational efficiency, environmental sustainability, and 
navigational safety.

the Q-learning framework. By leveraging BC for rapid policy initialization and Q-learning for autonomous exploration, KT-QL balances 
the strengths of both methods. This hybrid structure mitigates the compounding errors and limited generalization of BC, as well as the 
slow convergence of standard Q-learning, enabling the derivation of realistic and energy-efficient vessel routes.

2.2. Vessel trajectory planning approaches
Vessel trajectory prediction is crucial for ensuring safe navigation, preventing collisions, and optimizing operational efficiency. 
Over the years, various methodologies for this purpose have been developed and refined. These can be broadly categorized into four 
groups: (1) model-based optimization methods, (2) probabilistic and statistical prediction models, (3) ML and DL methods, and 
(4) RL frameworks.
(1) Model-based optimization methods include nonlinear programming, graph search algorithms such as A* and Dijkstra, and 
evolutionary algorithms such as genetic algorithms (GA) and particle swarm optimization (PSO) ( Djouani and Hamam, 1995 ; Alvarez 
et al., 2004 ; Niu et al., 2018 ). These methods are computationally efficient in static environments but show limited adaptability when 
dynamic conditions, such as changing weather or regulatory restrictions, must be considered. For instance, Alvarez et al. (2004)
employed GA for obstacle-avoidance routing in restricted waters but reported limitations in handling environmental variability.
(2) Statistical and probabilistic approaches aim to capture uncertainty in navigation dynamics. Kalman filter-based models are 
effective for real-time location and speed correction, whereas Markov chain models predict future trajectories using state-transition 
probabilities ( Perera et al., 2012 ; Qiao et al., 2015 ). Another common approach is similarity search, which predicts future routes 
by analyzing past trajectory patterns ( Hexeberg et al., 2017 ). Although these methods perform well in short-term forecasting, their 
excessive reliance on historical patterns often leads to poor generalizability when traffic or environmental distributions shift 
significantly.
(3) ML and DL methods have been applied to leverage the large-volume, time-series nature of AIS data. Classical supervised 
learning methods, such as k-nearest neighbors, support vector machines, and artificial neural networks, have been applied to capture 
nonlinear relationships between navigational features. Meanwhile, time-series neural networks such as long short-term memory 
(LSTM) and Transformer models have demonstrated strong performance in capturing sequential patterns ( Zhang et al., 2021 ; Nguyen 
and Fablet, 2024 ). Zhang et al. (2022) noted that LSTM-based models generally outperform traditional ML approaches and that 
incorporating attention mechanisms and multistage prediction architectures can further enhance performance. Nevertheless, these 
models may face challenges in generalizing to operational scenarios that are insufficiently represented in the training data, since they 
rely solely on supervised learning from observed AIS trajectories.
(4) RL frameworks have recently gained attention as they enable agents to learn navigation policies through trial-and-error 
interaction with dynamic environments. RL has been applied to route planning, estimated time of arrival (ETA) prediction, and 
fuel consumption optimization ( Chen et al., 2019 ; Guo et al., 2020 ; Moradi et al., 2022 ; Yoo and Kim, 2016 ; Sivaraj and Rajendran, 
2022 ). While classical Q-learning is conceptually simple, it often requires extensive training and may generate unrealistic paths 
without prior knowledge. To address these issues, recent studies have employed deep RL methods such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN), 
Double DQN (DDQN), Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Soft Actor-Critic. For example, 
Chen et al. (2025) combined A* search with DDQN to reduce fuel consumption and emissions, although performance remained highly 
sensitive to reward design. A recent preprint by Vaidheeswaran et al. (2025) introduced goal-conditioned RL (GCRL) with AIS-based 
traffic graphs and PPO variants, which improved adaptability across multiple routes but still suffered from data sparsity and limited

2.3. Knowledge transfer and imitation learning
KT and imitation learning have been explored as complementary strategies to improve the efficiency and adaptability of RL. These 
approaches can be broadly grouped into two categories: (1) transfer learning methods that reuse policies, features, or experiences 
across tasks, and (2) BC methods that directly mimic expert demonstrations.
(1) Transfer learning methods accelerate convergence and improve policy generalization by leveraging knowledge from related 
tasks ( Taylor and Stone, 2009 ; Lazaric, 2012 ). Many studies on RL have explored policy-based KT, where a policy learned in one 
environment is adapted to a new environment or a similar task. Bharadhwaj et al. (2019) proposed a sim-to-real transfer method 
wherein a policy trained within a simulation is deployed in a real-world environment. Zhang et al. (2017) accelerated policy learning 
by transferring state-reward structures using successor features. Teh et al. (2017) introduced a multitask learning framework (Distral) 
that learns task-specific policies while sharing a common representation across tasks. Parisotto et al. (2016) employed the actor – mimic 
architecture to demonstrate that a single policy can be effectively transferred to other tasks. Moreover, experience-based transfer 
methods that efficiently reuse past learning have been studied ( Yin and Pan, 2017 ; Barreto et al., 2017 ). While these methods improve 
sample efficiency, they are often developed in simulation environments and face challenges in real-world deployment, where negative 
transfer may occur if the source and target tasks differ significantly.
(2) BC is a form of imitation learning that uses supervised learning to directly mimic an expert ’ s decision-making process. By 
training on expert demonstrations, BC can replicate complex policies and generate effective initial policies in real-world settings, such 
as autonomous driving and energy control ( Ly and Akhloufi, 2021 ; Lee and Zhang, 2021 ). However, BC suffers from structural lim -
itations. Its performance suffers in unfamiliar states if the expert data are not comprehensive. Additionally, the learned policy is fixed 
and cannot adapt to changing environments. Early errors may also accumulate, leading to inefficient or unsafe behavior — a critical 
issue for tasks such as vessel route planning.
The proposed KT-QL method is designed to bridge the gaps between theoretical generalizability and practical applicability by 
combining the strengths of both categories. Unlike BC, which directly replicates expert demonstrations and suffers from poor 
adaptability in unfamiliar states, KT-QL leverages AIS-based expert trajectories only as an initialization step and subsequently refines 
policies through reward-driven Q-learning. In contrast to conventional policy transfer methods that generally rely on simulations or 
assume structural similarity across tasks, KT-QL directly embeds real-world AIS priors into the Q-learning framework, thereby 
improving operational realism. This hybrid design mitigates BC ’ s compounding errors and the unstable convergence of RL, enabling 
the derivation of vessel routes that are both computationally efficient and operationally viable. Moreover, it not only distinguishes KT- 
QL from conventional imitation learning and policy transfer methods but also provides several practical advantages that enhance its 
effectiveness for maritime trajectory planning, including the following:
• It accelerates convergence by initializing the policy with expert trajectories, thereby reducing the number of training episodes 
required.
• It improves generalization to unseen maritime conditions by combining data-driven priors with adaptive RL.
• It enhances operational realism and robustness by generating routes that reflect both empirical AIS traffic patterns and optimized 
energy efficiency.
Taken together, prior studies demonstrate a gradual evolution from optimization and statistical models to ML/DL, RL, and transfer- 
based approaches. Despite these advances, challenges remain in convergence stability, reward sensitivity, and the integration of expert 
navigational knowledge. Table 2 provides a consolidated comparison of representative methods across these categories, summarizing

3. Proposed approach
3.1. Overview of proposed approach
This section details the proposed vessel route planning method based on KT-QL. The core idea is to transfer expert navigation 
knowledge derived from real-world AIS data to a Q-learning agent. This KT is intended to improve the convergence speed of policy 
learning and enhance the agent ’ s ability to generate realistic vessel routes. As illustrated in Fig. 1 , the proposed method comprises the 
following four main steps:
Step 1: Environment setting 
• The RL environment is constructed based on vessel position data from AIS.
• This involves defining the agent ’ s state, action, and reward structure, including a reward and penalty function that reflects actual 
navigational constraints.
Step 2: Knowledge Engineering 
• Real-world navigation patterns, traffic densities, and avoidance tendencies are extracted from historical AIS trajectories.
• These features are formalized into a structured knowledge base, which serves as expert information for guiding RL.
Step 3: Baseline Q-learning 
• A Q-learning agent is trained on the training set with the Q-table initialized to zero, without KT.
• The learned Q-table is applied to the test set to generate trajectories representing the baseline outcome of experience-driven 
learning.
Step 4: KT-QL 
• The knowledge base from Step 2 is integrated into the Q-table obtained in Step 3 by adjusting Q-values with expert-derived 
preferences provided as supervisory signals.
• This process embeds expert knowledge into the previously learned Q-table, refining the Q-learning process under the same 
environment.

3.2. Environment setting
This step describes the construction of the RL environment based on AIS data. The environment is designed by converting vessel 
position information into nodes using the H3 grid system and then constructing a graph network where these nodes serve as the agent ’ s 
state-transition space.
3.2.1. Vessel location conversion to H3
To define the state space for the RL environment, the latitude and longitude coordinates recorded in AIS data were converted into 
the H3 spatial indexing system, an open-source framework developed by Uber Technologies (2019) . H3 is a hierarchical geospatial 
indexing system that divides the Earth ’ s surface into uniformly sized hexagonal cells. It provides a regularized and computationally 
efficient method for structuring and analyzing large-scale geospatial data. By transforming raw coordinate-based position data into H3 
hexagons, vessel trajectories can be represented as normalized state nodes, enabling the construction of a structured state space 
suitable for RL. This method has been widely employed in various location-based studies, including those on risk analysis, mobility 
modeling, and accessibility evaluation ( Aini et al., 2023 ).
In this study, raw position coordinates from AIS data were transformed into the H3 grid system. Each resulting hexagon cell was 
defined as a state in the RL environment, and a graph-based state-transition structure was subsequently established based on the 
connectivity and adjacency between neighboring nodes .
Fig. 2 visually compares the original vessel positions and their corresponding locations after the H3 conversion. The left panel 
illustrates the original vessel trajectory plotted in the raw coordinate system, whereas the right panel shows the same trajectory aligned 
to the centers of H3 hexagonal cells. This transformation restructures the discontinuous point data into an organized sequence of state 
nodes, thereby enabling the design of a graph-based learning environment. Through this conversion, the AIS-based RL environment 
becomes spatially regularized, laying the foundation for constructing a learnable state transition framework in which the agent can be 
trained.
3.2.2. Graph construction
Each vessel location, once converted into the H3 grid format, is defined as a node in a graph. In this structure, each node represents 
a vessel ’ s state, whereas the connections (edges) and their associated weights form the core of the state-transition model for the RL 
agent. During preprocessing, only sea-based AIS positions were retained before applying the H3 grid, which ensured that land areas 
were inherently excluded from the navigable state space. Consequently, the graph contains only maritime nodes, and land-based 
positions do not appear as candidates for agent transitions.
Previous studies have often defined the adjacency between nodes by physical distance ( Alessandrini et al., 2019 ; Park et al., 2021 ). 
Although this method enables distance-based route planning, it does not fully capture the realities of maritime navigation, where 
vessels frequently deviate from the shortest path owing to factors such as weather conditions, ocean currents, or route congestion. 
Furthermore, as the number of adjacent nodes increases, the search space expands rapidly, resulting in increased computational 
complexity.

3.5. Step 4: KT-QL
This section introduces the KT-QL procedure, which leverages expert trajectories extracted from AIS data to refine the policy 
learned through baseline Q-learning. Unlike the baseline model, which starts from a zero-initialized Q-table, KT-QL reuses the Q-table 
obtained in Section 3.4 as the starting point and further adjusts it by embedding expert-derived preferences. In this way, expert 
knowledge is integrated not as a replacement for baseline training, but as an additional supervisory signal that guides subsequent Q- 
value updates.
The goals of this KT method are as follows: 
• To refine the agent ’ s policy by reusing the baseline Q-table while incorporating expert trajectories as supervisory signals;
• To reduce unnecessary early-stage exploration and accelerate convergence;
• To steer the agent toward realistic route learning by embedding grounded navigation preferences.

5. Conclusion
5.1. Summary and contributions
This study proposed the KT-QL framework to enhance vessel route planning by overcoming the limitations of traditional RL 
methods. By embedding expert navigational knowledge extracted from AIS trajectories into the learning process, KT-QL addresses the 
slow convergence and limited operational realism of standard tabular Q-learning and improves the policy stability often challenged in 
deep RL (DQN) settings.
Comprehensive experiments demonstrated that KT-QL consistently outperformed both conventional Q-learning and Dijkstra-based 
search algorithms, and showed greater robustness than DQN in long-distance scenarios. The key findings and contributions are 
summarized as follows:
(1) Enhanced similarity to actual vessel routes : KT-QL produced trajectories with greater fidelity to actual vessel paths, reducing 
the Hausdorff distance by approximately 39 % in the ≥ 900 km range, thus improving route realism.
(2) Better alignment with maritime traffic patterns : KT-QL demonstrated a superior ability to capture principal navigation 
corridors, indicating that the model was not merely replicating observed data but was actively learning to identify optimal, high- 
density routes.
(3) Enhanced Energy-efficiency and stability : KT-QL generally achieved lower fuel-consumption errors than Q-learning across 
most voyage ranges, with improvements becoming more pronounced in medium- to long-distance scenarios. As summarized in 
Table 9, KT-QL reduced fuel-consumption error by approximately 37 % (RMSE) and 48 % (MAPE) compared with Q-learning in long- 
distance voyages ( ≥ 900 km). This demonstrates that the proposed model effectively learns energy-efficient navigation policies, 
particularly for extended voyages where conventional Q-learning tends to diverge.
These findings suggest that KT-QL effectively integrates expert maritime knowledge with RL, leading to policies that are more 
realistic and energy-efficient than conventional methods. The proposed framework advances data-driven route-planning research and 
may serve as a foundation for future development of adaptive, fuel-efficient decision-support systems in maritime operations.
5.2. Theoretical and managerial implications
Theoretically, this study contributes to RL research by demonstrating how KT can enhance model performance in complex and 
uncertain environments. The KT-QL framework illustrates the potential of integrating expert domain knowledge to improve 
convergence stability and policy generalization, thereby extending the scope of knowledge-driven RL beyond controlled simulations. 
These findings open new directions for exploring hybrid learning paradigms that combine human expertise and autonomous adap -
tation in sequential decision-making problems.
From a managerial perspective, KT-QL provides meaningful insights for maritime operations and route-planning practice. By 
producing more realistic and energy-efficient trajectories that align with existing navigation corridors, the approach supports data- 
driven decision-making toward more sustainable and cost-effective fleet management. Although further validation in operational 
settings is required, KT-QL may serve as a foundational concept for developing intelligent decision-support systems aimed at improving 
voyage planning and energy management in maritime logistics.

5.3. Limitations and future research directions
Although the proposed KT-QL model demonstrated substantial improvements, it has some limitations that should be addressed in 
future research: 
• Environmental and regulatory factors : The current model does not explicitly consider dynamic environmental or regulatory 
conditions such as ocean currents, weather disturbances, port congestion, piracy zones, or emission control areas. Moreover, 
decarbonization-related constraints — such as speed limits or fuel-emission policies — were not embedded into the reward 
structure. Future studies should extend the state-action space to reflect these dynamic and policy-driven elements, for example, by 
penalizing entries into restricted zones or modeling regulatory compliance as a state-dependent reward. Incorporating such factors 
would enhance both the safety and regulatory robustness of the trajectory-planning process.
• Generalizability : The experiments were conducted under specific geographical and vessel conditions. To ensure robustness, 
broader validation across multiple maritime regions, vessel classes, and varying traffic densities is needed. Such cross-domain 
evaluation would clarify how effectively the KT-QL framework generalizes to different operational contexts.
• Real-time adaptability : The present framework relies on historical AIS data and offline training. Future research should develop 
continuous or online learning mechanisms that adapt to real-time AIS streams, weather updates, and port-state information. 
Combining RL with streaming data assimilation could enable dynamic trajectory updates responsive to evolving maritime 
environments.
To strengthen the theoretical foundation and practical applicability of KT-QL, several promising extensions can be envisioned: 
• Multi-vessel coordination: Extending the framework to cooperative routing could improve fleet-level traffic management 
and congestion avoidance.
• Simulator and field validation : Testing the model in high-fidelity maritime simulators and controlled sea trials would provide 
empirical evidence of operational feasibility and safety.
• Integration of environmental and policy data : Embedding real-time weather, wave, current, and decarbonization constraints 
directly into the learning process would enhance both adaptability and compliance.
By pursuing these directions, KT-QL can evolve into a robust, adaptive, and regulation-aware route-planning framework that 
bridges the gap between methodological innovation and practical maritime operation.
