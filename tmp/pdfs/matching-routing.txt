 A B S T R A C T
Integrating smart technologies into freight operations is essential for achieving efficiency, 
sustainability, and cost-effectiveness in modern logistics. This research presents a novel smart 
freight platform to optimize matching and routing in freight logistics. The platform incorporates 
sequential matching and a dynamic bidding mechanism, including Pre-filter matching, Main 
matching, and Non-Contracted Shippers (NCS) matching models. It utilizes the Vehicle Routing 
Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW) model to align delivery schedules with shippers’ time 
windows. The proposed platform reduces resource consumption by minimizing empty truck 
routes through NCS alignment with en-route trucks. In particular, empty truck routes were 
reduced by %39, while gas emissions decreased by over nine tons daily. Therefore, the 
proposed platform not only improves freight efficiency but also contributes to environmental 
sustainability.
1. Introduction
In the modern logistics and transportation industry, achieving efficiency, optimality, and environmental sustainability is 
more important than ever. These factors are critical, especially in addressing matching, pricing, and routing challenges. Freight 
transportation faces significant challenges in reducing carbon emissions and optimizing operations. The growing complexity of 
logistics networks has led to the development of new business models designed to address these issues. These models ensure resource 
efficiency and seamless integration between organizations while minimizing environmental impact (Padidar et al., 2023). Cargo 
transportation has become a growing challenge, particularly with the continuous expansion of the world’s urban population. In 
2018, over half of the global population lived in urban areas, and it is projected that more than two-thirds (68.7%) will reside in 
metropolitan areas by mid-century (Li et al., 2022). With a large population concentrated in cities, the demand for efficient freight 
systems will be critical in addressing urban logistical challenges. Shippers often encounter obstacles when working with multiple 
carriers, including service quality variability, pricing negotiations, and transparency concerns. These challenges highlight the need 
for a smart freight platform that promotes collaboration and integration. Such a platform would integrate matching, pricing, and 
routing solutions to optimize the transportation processes (Mukhopadhyay and Setaputra, 2006).
The matching problem in freight transportation arises from the interaction between the commodity and the freight market (Ro-
orda et al., 2010; Cavalcante and Roorda, 2013). The evolution of the internet and information technology has enabled the 
development of platforms for sharing freight resources. This also introduces a new business model for the freight industry, enhancing

interactions with offline services. It improves resource efficiency by reducing empty vehicle trips. Freight transportation exchanges

By minimizing vehicle usage and optimizing shipments, matching systems reduce both operational costs and fuel consumption,

are often overlooked. The challenge lies in finding ways to further integrate environmental concerns into this process to reduce

Traditionally, distribution matching strategies have been widely used without integrating pricing mechanisms (Li et al., 2020; 
Mei et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020b) or considering environmental concerns (Song et al., 2024; Furuhata et al., 2013). Developing 
an effective pricing strategy in a smart freight platform is challenging due to the complexities of balancing supply and demand. 
However, achieving such a smart platform maximizes operational efficiency and profitability. As a solution, dynamic pricing models 
adjust prices based on real-time demand patterns (Pfrommer et al., 2014; Jiao et al., 2020). These models provide flexibility in 
responding to market competition. They also offer price incentives that encourage sustainable behaviors (Xu et al., 2018; Yan

considerations. Innovative pricing strategies can adapt to market conditions and user preferences in the dynamic transportation 
landscape (De Ruijter et al., 2023). Moreover, they are crucial for reducing operational costs while improving system efficiency (van 
Heeswijk, 2022; Özkan, 2020).
Routing in freight transportation presents another significant challenge. Factors like vehicle capacity, delivery timing, and traffic 
conditions must be considered to ensure cost-effective and eco-friendly routes (Nam et al., 2013; Birol, 2009). Poor routing not only 
increases fuel consumption but also contributes significantly to greenhouse gas emissions. In response, municipalities are increasingly 
prioritizing smarter routing solutions to alleviate urban congestion and reduce the environmental impact of freight transport (Demir 
et al., 2015; Enthoven et al., 2020). Controlling emissions from heavy-duty vehicles (HDVs), which contribute disproportionately to 
pollution, is a top priority. Reducing these emissions is crucial for improving air quality and addressing environmental concerns (Lv 
et al., 2023). Without effective measures, global 𝐶𝑂 2 emissions could increase by 40% by 2030, reaching 40 billion tonnes per 
year (Nam et al., 2013; Birol, 2009). Advanced routing methods can help optimize vehicle use, reduce emissions, and improve 
overall sustainability. Understanding the roles of stakeholders in freight transportation and how they interact is crucial for developing 
public sector models. Additionally, analyzing how these interactions change over time helps in creating models that focus on efficient 
resource consumption and sustainable solutions (Roorda et al., 2010).
While advancements in freight transportation models and smart platforms have improved efficiency and resource utilization, 
significant gaps remain. Many existing solutions address matching, pricing, and routing as separate challenges, often neglecting 
their integration with environmental sustainability goals. Current matching strategies focus on cost reduction but rarely incorporate 
mechanisms to align with pricing and sustainability objectives. Pricing strategies, though dynamic, often lack incentives to promote 
eco-friendly practices. Similarly, routing methods optimize delivery times and costs but frequently overlook their environmental 
impact, such as reducing greenhouse gas emissions or mitigating urban congestion. These challenges highlight the need for an 
integrated approach that combines matching, pricing, and routing while embedding sustainability at the core of freight logistics. To 
address these gaps, this paper introduces several novel contributions aimed at improving efficiency, optimizing resource allocation, 
and enhancing environmental sustainability:
• Integrating matching and routing with a dynamic bidding mechanism to enhance the efficiency of the matching process within 
a smart freight platform.
• Introducing an integrated sequential matching approach that comprises three stages — Pre-filter matching, Main matching, 
and NCS matching — to effectively address diverse matching parameters.
• Leveraging H3 hexagon grids to locate the nearest trucks to shippers, improving spatial accuracy and reducing response times.
• Introducing a consolidation approach for Non-Contracted Shippers (NCS) to maximize resource utilization and minimize 
inefficiencies.
• Ensuring scalability to support both intra-city and inter-city freight logistics, accommodating varying levels of demand and 
geographical complexity.
While the platform inherently improves cost efficiency through route optimization and reduced travel distances, this study 
intentionally emphasizes environmental sustainability as a primary objective. In conventional fuel-based freight systems, emissions, 
fuel use, and operational costs are tightly linked. By focusing on strategies like en-route truck consolidation and real-time matching 
of NCS requests, we demonstrate that reducing emissions also results in indirect cost savings. For example, decreasing empty truck 
routing leads to fewer trips, reduced fuel consumption, and improved vehicle utilization, all of which contribute to lower operational 
costs. Rather than contradicting cost-efficiency goals, our sustainability-oriented approach complements them. The results show that 
prioritizing ecological performance, such as reducing 𝐶𝑂 2 by over 9 tons per day, not only supports environmental targets but also 
strengthens platform profitability and delivery capacity. This dual benefit highlights the value of embedding sustainability into 
logistical decision-making and supports a new standard for freight platform development.

2. Related works
The advent of smart freight platforms has revolutionized the logistics industry, introducing advanced technologies that have 
transformed traditional freight operations. These platforms are at the forefront of integrating digital innovations into logistics 
processes. They offer efficient and adaptable solutions to meet the evolving demands of modern supply chains. A key feature of these 
platforms is a matching system, which leverages advanced algorithms to optimize the pairing of shippers with carriers, ensuring 
deliveries are both cost-effective and timely. Moreover, the VRPTW integration has further enhanced the platforms’ ability to manage 
complex logistics networks. VRPTW is an essential tool for logistics optimization, addressing the challenge of determining the most 
efficient routes for vehicles to meet delivery schedules within specified time windows. This improves service reliability and reduces 
operational inefficiencies, such as unnecessary travel and idle time.
This literature review will explore the development and application of these technologies, focusing on their contributions to 
optimizing freight logistics. By examining the integration of matching algorithms and VRPTW in smart freight platforms, the review 
aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of how these innovations drive both economic and environmental benefits in the 
logistics sector.
2.1. Smart freight platform
The integration of advanced technologies in freight transportation has transformed operational strategies and market dynamics. 
A significant development in this area is the adoption of agent-based modeling frameworks, which provide a robust platform for 
analyzing logistics services and interactions within the freight system (Roorda et al., 2010). These frameworks highlight the roles 
of diverse actors and the establishment of contracts within the market (Roorda et al., 2010).
Freight transportation, a crucial part of global supply chains, encompasses a broad range of topics, including modeling, pricing, 
negative externalities, transportation network design, and sustainable practices (Demir et al., 2015). The literature on transportation 
has also focused on mitigating the effects of disruptions that cause deviations from the initial transport plans. Most approaches to 
managing disruptions in road freight transport concentrate on urban freight distributions, where disruptions are often handled by 
vehicle rerouting (Karam and Reinau, 2022). For instance, Karam and Reinau (2022) presented a hybrid method that integrates 
simulation modeling, optimization algorithms, and cost–benefit analyses. This method swiftly evaluates the impact of disruptions, 
identifies affected journeys, and promptly adjusts plans in real-time. Six alternative planning strategies are introduced to manage 
disruptions, with evaluations based on cost, reliability (measured by time delays), and CO2 emissions.
Another innovation is the development of a shared Freight Service Intelligence Platform (FSIP) that leverages telematics-enabled 
Freight Transport Assets (FTA) to meet the needs of various stakeholders in the freight industry (Heinbach et al., 2022). This 
smart platform helps transportation professionals coordinate shipments by tracking performance and identifying unusual patterns. 
It also assesses risks, shares data, facilitates communication, and integrates with other systems. From this study, five key principles 
were developed to address the main needs identified. The tool was tested by six transport experts and logistics software providers, 
showcasing its potential in enhancing coordination across the freight industry.
Further, (Dai et al., 2023) explored how prices are balanced on online retail platforms, examining whether premium or budget 
items are better suited for direct sales or resale. The study found that product quality, influenced by competition between channels, 
commission rates, and product costs, plays a significant role in decision-making. In some cases, product quality can change how 
suppliers make decisions, potentially leading to mutually beneficial outcomes for the supplier, the platform, and the customers. 
Following the discussion of the smart freight platform, the next section explores how the matching problem addresses critical 
challenges in logistics.
2.2. Matching problem
The two-sided matching problem, first introduced by Gale and Shapley (1962), is an assignment problem that involves two 
distinct sets of entities, each with its own preferences for members of the opposite group (Gale and Shapley, 1962). This research 
is the basis for various matching markets, where stable matching principles ensure equitable and optimal outcomes (Ausseil, 
2022). In the context of transportation platforms, dynamic methods for matching requests to suppliers explore the complexity of 
matching algorithms. These methods optimize transportation networks using algorithms such as preference-aware task assignment, 
ride-sourcing modeling, and dynamic pricing strategies (Ausseil, 2022).
The importance of fairness in repeated matchings within two-sided markets is highlighted in a case study on a ride-hailing 
platform. The study emphasizes the need for equitable treatment of both drivers and riders to optimize platform operations and 
improve overall satisfaction. Özkan (2020) investigated the interplay between pricing and matching decisions on ride-sharing 
platforms, emphasizing the need to jointly optimize both factors to achieve optimal performance. Similarly, a mathematical 
framework is presented by Yan et al. (2021) to integrate matching and pricing decisions, aiming to achieve optimal ride matching 
while ensuring system stability and financial sustainability.

The matching process in ride-hailing systems has also been studied by Li and Liu (2021), particularly examining the feasibility 
of one-to-two matching strategies. This research introduced an incentive scheme and pricing model that considers riders’ bounded 
rational behavior, offering insights into enhancing platform efficiency. In the context of autonomous vehicle systems, (Allahviranloo 
and Bouyeure, 2022) designed and tested an online platform for shared autonomous vehicles (SAVs). This platform allows 
households to co-own vehicles, reserve time slots, and trade reservations with other co-owners. This model incorporates preferences,

In the cargo market, price matching has also been a focus. Xu et al. (2021) analyzed the impact of price-matching policies on

through dynamic cargo stowage. To reach this goal, they introduced a model that predicts vehicle arrival times and adjusts stowage

ratio to optimize the allocation of vehicles between pooling and non-pooling orders. The study identified conditions under which 
en-route ride-pooling services could benefit all stakeholders (Wang et al., 2025). Having addressed the matching problem, the next 
section focuses on the pricing strategies employed in transportation services.
2.3. Pricing
The study of pricing strategies in transport systems has attracted significant attention, particularly in the context of inter-modal 
freight transport and ride-sharing platforms. Li et al. (2015) explored the complexities of pricing in inter-modal freight transport, 
proposing a cost-plus pricing strategy that incorporates operational costs and targeted profit margins. Their study also addressed 
challenges faced by operators, such as traffic congestion and limited infrastructure capacity, suggesting that well-designed pricing 
strategies can enhance competitiveness and influence mode choice. The significance of accurate cost calculation and market insights 
was underscored as a critical factor in developing effective pricing approaches for inter-modal transport services.
In the context of ride-sharing, (Özkan, 2020; Yan et al., 2021) examined the interplay between pricing and matching to 
optimize system performance. The former emphasized the need for joint optimization of pricing and matching decisions to achieve 
maximum matching rates, noting that simple pricing strategies can be effective under certain conditions. Conversely, (Yan et al., 
2021) highlighted the role of dynamic pricing models in accommodating diverse consumer behaviors and improving ride-sharing 
efficiency. Additionally, studies on dynamic methods, including passenger-based and distance-based pricing approaches, support 
the recommendation of applying synchronized optimization models for pricing strategies in taxi-sharing services (Qiu et al., 2022). 
Further, considering the strategic interactions between traditional taxi services (TTS) and online car-hailing (OCS) during the 
COVID-19 pandemic, (Gu et al., 2024) applied Game Theory to analyze the dynamics between these two systems.
Walunj et al. (2022) investigated market segmentation in ride-hailing, illustrating how different pricing strategies influence 
competition and consumer choices. They showed that price differentiation based on origin and destination can significantly impact 
the total number of matches and overall system efficiency. Additionally, (De Ruijter et al., 2023) explored the impact of alternative 
demand behaviors and pricing settings on ride-pooling adoption and service levels. They presented a graph-based assignment method 
for optimizing the matching of requests to vehicles while considering vehicle capacity and travel cost constraints. Another study 
evaluated collaboration and profit-sharing methods for carriers, aiming to improve operational efficiency in pricing and ensure 
fair profit distribution in pickup-and-delivery problems using Game Theory (Padmanabhan et al., 2023). Pricing strategies for 
internalizing these externalities have also been explored to align economic activities with environmental sustainability (Demir et al., 
2015; Nam et al., 2013).
In the context of Electric Vehicle Sharing (EVS), (Jiao et al., 2020) introduced a price-incentive method aimed at addressing the 
imbalance in EVS systems. This approach adjusts rental prices on mobile terminals to control users’ trip decisions. Operators reduce 
return prices at low-demand stations with idle vehicles to attract customers and improve vehicle utilization and rental revenue. 
Conversely, at stations with high demand for vehicles and parking spots, operators raise return prices to reduce customer adoption 
and maintain a manageable demand level while increasing profits. This dynamic pricing strategy optimizes both service adoption 
and operational profitability by adjusting trip prices based on vehicle availability and demand imbalances. Following the discussion 
on pricing strategies, the next section explores how the VRPTW framework addresses operational challenges in delivery services.
2.4. Vehicle routing problem
The vehicle routing problem (VRP) is a critical area of research in logistics and transportation management, focusing on 
optimizing vehicle routes and schedules to service customers within specific time frames. A VRP solver has been applied in 
freight transport to enhance the efficiency of urban consolidation centers (UCC) by minimizing costs, travel distances, and carbon 
emissions, particularly in the context of student accommodations (Bukoye and Gadiraju, 2022). The VRPTW extends the classical 
VRP by incorporating time constraints for deliveries and pickups, making it more applicable to real-world scenarios where timing is 
crucial (Solomon, 1987). Early research by Solomon (1987) introduced the VRPTW, highlighting its relevance in various practical 
applications such as postal deliveries, school bus routing, and industrial refuse collection. This study emphasized the challenges 
of integrating time windows into routing and scheduling, which require balancing travel costs, vehicle utilization, and customer 
satisfaction (Solomon, 1987).
Negative externalities, such as emissions, noise pollution, and congestion, have been a major focus of freight transportation 
research. Studies have proposed various models to quantify these externalities and assess their impact across different transport 
modes, including road, rail, maritime, and air (Demir et al., 2015).

3. Problem statement
On the cutting edge of transforming logistics and freight management, matching and routing problems are central to the evolution 
of how goods are transported. To enhance both the performance and environmental impact of cargo transportation, it is essential to 
tackle matching and route problems effectively. In the traditional approach, a shipper, who owns cargo and seeks delivery, must find 
a carrier with a suitable truck. After identifying a potential match, the process typically involves time-consuming price negotiations, 
which may not always lead to an agreement. If no agreement is reached, the shipper must restart the search.
To address these challenges effectively, a robust optimization method is required that can adapt to large-scale market fluctuations. 
This method should be agile enough to re-optimize solutions as circumstances evolve. Such flexibility is key to maintaining the 
balance between competitive pricing and profit maximization, which are critical for long-term business viability in a competitive 
market.
Taxi-hailing platforms such as Uber match customers and drivers to provide seamless rides. By leveraging this approach, we 
optimize the matching process, ensuring that shippers receive reliable and timely delivery services while carriers maximize service 
quality, capacity utilization, and profitability. However, the smart freight platform faces several challenges that must be addressed to 
enhance overall performance and sustainability. For the rest of this document, we will refer to the smart freight platform simply as 
the platform. The primary challenge is to enhance the matching efficiency between shippers and carriers. The diversity in cargo types, 
weights, and truck availability requires a novel matching algorithm to ensure optimal pairings. A significant issue is the presence of 
empty trucks en route, leading to wasted resources and increased operational costs. Reducing these instances is crucial for improving

4. Methodology
The nature of the matching problem is highly complex, making it challenging to analyze and process all relevant features 
simultaneously. To address this, we use sequential mathematical models that break down the problem into smaller, more manageable 
steps. These models are based on the concept of sequential processes, allowing for the consideration of multiple features in a 
logical order. This method enables a comprehensive understanding of the problem, leading to more accurate and efficient matching 
outcomes. We also address the VRPTW to prioritize transportation routes on our platform. This approach not only increases control 
over the entire delivery process but also helps us generate sustainable solutions.
To represent spatial information in the urban freight environment, we adopt the H3 hexagonal grid system rather than 
a traditional radius-based (circular) approach. Unlike circular methods, H3 partitions the service region into contiguous, non-
overlapping hexagonal cells of uniform size. This eliminates problems of overlap and gaps common in radius-based or square-grid 
systems, which often complicate distance calculations and lead to inconsistent spatial indexing. The uniformity and adjacency 
features of H3 greatly improve the platform’s ability to identify nearby trucks without requiring direct distance computations for 
each request. Furthermore, H3 supports hierarchical resolution levels, allowing the system to dynamically scale search areas, starting 
from fine-grained local searches and expanding outward if necessary, without recalculating coordinates or introducing additional 
computational cost. This is particularly advantageous in dense urban settings such as Montreal, where delivery decisions must be 
both fast and spatially precise. In practical terms, the city is preprocessed once into a hexagonal grid, and each shipper or carrier 
is mapped to a corresponding cell. This structure avoids repetitive calculations for each new request and accelerates the matching 
process significantly. Therefore, in a dynamic logistics environment where thousands of matches must be evaluated quickly, H3 
offers both computational speed and spatial resolution that radius-based methods lack. This makes it well-suited for real-time freight 
platforms.
To ensure fair pricing, we implement a bidding process that allows carriers to bid for shipments, promoting competitive pricing 
that benefits both shippers and carriers while maintaining fairness in the transaction. Our platform is designed to accommodate two 
distinct types of shippers — Contracted Shippers (CS) and Non-Contracted Shippers (NCS) — to address different needs within the 
freight logistics ecosystem. This flexibility allows the platform to cater to a broader range of users. Moreover, the platform offers end-
to-end delivery management, overseeing the entire operation from routing to consolidation. This comprehensive approach ensures 
a seamless and integrated freight delivery experience, providing greater control over the logistics process. Finally, the method is 
scalable, making it applicable to both intra-city and inter-city freight logistics. This scalability ensures that the platform can handle 
a wide range of freight operations, regardless of the geographical scope.
The proposed method consists of four sequential steps based on real-world scenarios that occur on the platform, which includes 
the mathematical model and processes. The process begins with the generation of shipping requests on the platform, including the 
required shipping data. This data is then promptly matched with available drivers based on their locations and schedules, creating 
a responsive and efficient freight management system. There are three main stakeholders involved — shippers, carriers, and the
platform itself — each defined by their own unique characteristics (See Table  2).
In the proposed model, shippers are divided into two distinct groups: CS and NCS. This classification improves the model’s 
transparency and allows the platform to address the specific needs of each group. CS are those who have concluded a formal contract 
with the platform. These shippers expect their cargo to be delivered by a carrier while considering factors such as preferred quality 
and competitive pricing. Typically, CS deals with a single pick-up point for cargo but may have multiple delivery points. We allow 
a truck to serve one CS along with multiple NCS, leveraging en-route consolidation to increase efficiency and reduce empty travel. 
Similarly, each truck is generally assigned to serve one primary CS shipment, potentially supplemented by smaller NCS requests en 
route. NCS, on the other hand, does not hold an active contract with the platform, but can still receive its services. NCS, however, 
may not have access to the same delivery preferences as CS. NCS requests usually include one pick-up point and a single delivery 
point, which will be serviced by an en-route truck. It is important to mention that the platform previously assigned idle trucks for 
such deliveries.
The delivery price for CS will be determined through a bidding process, whereas the delivery charges for NCS may not be as 
competitive as those for CS. Moreover, offering fair pricing for CS can motivate NCS to formalize contracts with the platform, thereby 
fostering stronger partnerships and improving service consistency. In terms of carriers, they own various types of trucks to serve 
delivery services for shippers. Once a carrier is recognized as eligible by the platform, it is allowed to participate in the bidding 
process to compete with other carriers for delivery assignments. The eligibility process for carriers will be discussed in Section 4.2.
The platform itself serves as the central entity that matches carriers with shippers. It also manages the entire delivery operation, 
from generating shipping requests and facilitating the bidding process to fulfillment of the delivery. The platform ensures that all 
deliveries meet the required criteria, thereby optimizing the logistics process. To address the challenges presented by the matching 
problem, four models have been developed. These models include Pre-filter matching, Main matching, NCS matching, and VRPTW. 
Each of these models plays a crucial role in navigating the platform’s operations and ensuring that generated requests are effectively 
addressed.

4.1. Mathematical formulation
We formulate the matching problem as a sequential matching model, which enhances the efficiency of the VRPTW model in 
the transportation stage. This approach reduces the complexity of the problem and refines matching parameters that need to be 
considered. In addition to the VRPTW model, this study develops three models — Pre-filter matching, Main matching, and NCS 
matching — to manage the entire delivery operation. The models rely on several key assumptions. For distance estimation, we use 
Google Maps H3 hexagons to calculate the distance between two points, as will be discussed in greater detail later. Carriers have 
the flexibility to offer varying prices based on factors such as truck type and quality score. Each truck is capable of serving multiple 
requests, enabling efficient utilization of resources. Delivery operations are designed to adapt dynamically to evolving scenarios, 
such as new requests from NCS, allowing point prioritization to shift as needed. The operation starts from a single origin point, 
typically the shipper’s location, and concludes at the carrier’s garage. It is structured around the shipper’s preferred time windows 
to ensure timely service. However, as delivery time is less critical for NCS, time windows are automatically disregarded. The arcs 
between two delivery points are unidirectional, and each delivery point must be visited once to ensure optimal routing. At the end 
of the delivery process, shippers provide a service score to evaluate their satisfaction with the service received. This score will be 
taken into account as a quality score for the carrier or truck and will influence the matching process for future delivery requests. The

The first approach, known as Origin and Destination-based Pricing (ODP), is utilized during the bidding process to calculate the 
maximum price 𝑝𝑚𝑎𝑥
𝑠 . This pricing strategy is based on the distance between the origin and destination hexagons. The calculation 
takes into account the number of hexagons that the truck must pass through to complete the delivery operation. The ODP method 
focuses on key parameters such as the shipper’s origin, the delivery destinations the truck must reach, and associated costs, including 
fuel, labor, vehicle maintenance, and insurance (Holguín-Veras and Patil, 2008; Li et al., 2015). For requests that bypass the bidding 
process, we employ the Origin-based Pricing (OP) strategy, which determines the price primarily based on the shipper’s origin. Any 
detour from the pre-defined Delivery Operation Plan (DOP) can disrupt operations. In this scenario, the risk of deviating from 
designated time windows increases, especially for trucks already in service. Shipper’s origin is vital when determining the price. 
Then, for the requests without a bidding process, the OP method is used. The OP method also accounts for additional expenditure, 
including a three percent surcharge for the deviation rate and a four percent increase for requests without a contract (Özkan, 2020; 
Li et al., 2015). This careful consideration of the shipper’s origin ensures the fulfillment of requests through en-route trucking, 
particularly for NCS, whose origin is a pivotal factor in determining pricing. In the next section, the mathematical models are 
presented. Table  3 provides the mathematical notation for the associated models.
4.2. Pre-filter matching
The Pre-filtering matching process aims to identify carriers with the potential to initiate the bidding process by evaluating several 
key parameters. The first parameter is region, where we utilize the H3 approach1 to identify the nearest truck belonging to a 
specific carrier within the shipper’s region. The H3 approach combines spatial indexing techniques with hierarchical data structures, 
providing an efficient and precise method to locate the most suitable truck. The shipper’s location is identified using a hexagonal grid 
and selects trucks located in one of the six neighboring hexagons (Fig.  1). The selected truck must belong to the specified carrier and 
meet the requirements outlined by the shipper. The second parameter is truck availability, which considers the operational status 
of the potential trucks and evaluates the estimated time required to complete the delivery operation. Only trucks available for the 
required timeframe are considered. The third parameter is truck capacity, where each truck’s predetermined capacity is assessed. 
Trucks that have the appropriate capacity to carry the specified cargo are included in the selection.

hexagonal cells at varying resolutions. This approach offers a hierarchical representation of geographic data. For our purposes, we utilize resolution layer 7 to 
precisely identify the location of each object, leveraging unique hexagonal identifiers to facilitate spatial analysis and proximity searches.

In some instances, the Main matching model may not yield a unique result. This occurs when multiple carriers and trucks meet 
all defined criteria, such as compatibility with cargo, sufficient capacity, compliance with quality thresholds, and availability within 
the required time window. In such cases, the platform applies a structured decision-making strategy to resolve the ambiguity and 
finalize a unique match. First, the platform uses a proximity-based selection method grounded in a high-resolution H3 hexagonal 
spatial grid. This technique allows for the precise localization of shippers and available trucks. Among the candidate matches, the 
truck located closest to the shipper, based on the H3 spatial resolution, is selected. If multiple trucks fall within the same closest 
hexagon, the proximity criterion alone is insufficient to determine a unique match.

bid that still satisfies all operational constraints is selected, thereby ensuring cost-effectiveness without compromising quality or 
compatibility. If, after applying both proximity and price criteria, more than one eligible truck remains, the final selection is made 
randomly. This tiered resolution process ensures fairness and maintains platform responsiveness under conditions of non-unique 
solutions.
4.4. VRPTW
The primary objective of the VRPTW model is to minimize both travel time and cost. Each delivery point is associated with 
a specific time window, denoted as [𝐸𝑖, 𝐿𝑖], where 𝐸 represents the earliest possible departure time and 𝐿 indicates the latest 
permissible arrival time. These time windows are predetermined by the shipper and specified while generating the delivery request 
on the platform. Through following the VRPTW model, the delivery operation begins from the shipper’s origin and delivers cargo to 
multiple points within the specific time window (Toth and Vigo, 2002). The model produces an optimized delivery plan referred to as 
the designed DOP. Each delivery point must be visited exactly once within its designated time window, and the operation concludes 
at the truck’s garage. To ensure a sustainable solution, truck capacity information is incorporated into the model, enabling the 
management of partially occupied and empty trucks. Managing truck capacity effectively allows for consolidation, ensuring that 
when a truck has sufficient capacity to handle additional requests during the operation, the platform can optimize resource usage. 
This leads to reduced traffic, lower 𝐶𝑂 2 emissions, and time savings. A consolidation constraint is therefore integrated into the 
VRPTW model to address truck capacity, contributing to a more efficient and environmentally friendly delivery process.

model is re-run to accommodate the new pickup and delivery points. The DOP is updated accordingly, and the operations proceed 
based on the revised or pre-designed plan. The process concludes when the delivery operation is successfully completed, ensuring 
efficient resource utilization, minimized empty truck routing, and adherence to time constraints.

5. Experiments
The case study focuses on Shiphual, a Montreal-based Canadian company, within the framework of a smart freight platform. 
This platform is designed to optimize freight matching and routing by incorporating various stakeholders and their respective 
attributes. It has established contracts with 300 shippers, ensuring their cargo is matched with the most suitable carriers and trucks for 
delivery. Additionally, 100 shippers without contracts generate ad-hoc requests periodically, which the platform fulfills by identifying 
uncommitted trucks available for delivery. The platform operates through a network of 100 carriers, each managing a fleet of at least 
five trucks across three types — box, flatbed, and tanker — offering diverse transportation options and ensuring efficient service 
coverage. To validate the model’s feasibility and constraint handling, we first tested it on a smaller dataset comprising 50 shippers, 
20 carriers, and 3 trucks. Once the model demonstrated reliable performance, we applied it to a full-scale dataset reflective of the 
company’s actual operations. The full dataset includes 400 delivery requests (300 CS and 100 NCS), 100 carriers, and a total of 500
trucks (five per carrier, distributed among the three truck types). The largest scenario included 1,400 delivery requests (1,000 from 
CS and 400 from NCS) and 100 carriers, each managing 50 trucks. Additional scenarios included varying the number of shippers, 
carriers, and trucks to test performance under unbalanced supply–demand conditions—for example, cases with more shippers than 
trucks and vice versa. These stress tests demonstrated that the model adapts well and maintains reasonable execution times, even 
under heavy computational loads.

efficiency by making full use of available resources.
The scatter plot in Fig.  6 illustrates that for all scenarios of shipper requests, the routing cost increased after applying the

unconsolidated case. This significant increase in serving demand is a notable operational achievement and directly contributes to 
increased revenue. Although shipping costs increased per unit, total revenue increased as the number of requests processed increased 
by 39%. It means that more demand was successfully matched and satisfied. Additionally, this consolidation results in fewer empty 
or underutilized trucks and spreads operating fixed costs across more shipments. This reduces the cost of each delivery from the 
carriers’ perspective, maintaining or improving their profit margins even at slightly higher expense. Moreover, the platform benefits 
from increased transactions and improved match rate, which are typically associated with better platform performance.

resource utilization and reducing environmental impact. A key component of this strategy involves applying NCS matching for 
en-route trucks. This approach has significantly enhanced resource utilization and reduced the frequency of empty truck travel. 
Previously, NCS requests were matched with idle or unused trucks, which often resulted in each truck serving a single request, even 
if it had unused capacity while en route. This inefficiency not only led to suboptimal resource management but also contributed to

In summary, the reported 39% reduction in empty truck routing is not only a technical optimization but a measurable 
contributor to fuel savings, emissions reduction, and improved operational efficiency. It reflects a systemic shift from reactive to 
proactive logistics planning, transforming how the platform leverages real-time capacity and positioning to deliver economic and 
environmental benefits.
6. Conclusion
This study proposes a new smart freight platform designed to optimize matching and routing, while improving environmental 
sustainability. By reducing the number of empty trucks operating en route, the platform helped alleviate urban congestion and 
supported community well-being, amplifying its positive ecological and societal contributions.
Social and economic impact The introduction of a sequential matching mechanism significantly improved system accuracy and 
resource efficiency. A dynamic bidding strategy for pre-filtered carriers enhanced the fairness and competitiveness of the pricing 
process. This incentivized NCS to engage with the platform and promoted equitable service conditions for all participants. Aligning 
NCS requests with en-route trucks helped reduce the number of empty trips, which in turn decreased fuel consumption, gas emissions, 
and urban traffic congestion while boosting stakeholder satisfaction and overall profitability. The platform’s social and economic 
impact was further reinforced by minimizing the need for idle vehicle deployment.
Environmental and economic benefits The operational enhancements led to a 39% reduction in empty truck routing, which translated

usage. Based on standard emissions estimates, 20 kg 𝐶𝑂 2 per truck per hour, a 12-hour reduction in 39 empty truck trips equates to

Limitation and future research The results validate the model’s success and provide a foundation for future innovation in urban freight 
systems. However, the study’s findings also highlight some limitations. First, the model is constrained to the local infrastructure data, 
demand patterns, and regulatory conditions specific to the area. These contextual factors may limit the direct transferability of results 
to other geographical locations with differing operational environments, traffic dynamics, or policy frameworks. Second, the truck 
classification used in this study is limited to three types — box, flatbed, and tanker — reflecting the most common vehicle types 
in our dataset. While sufficient for the studied context, this classification may not capture the full diversity of logistics fleets in 
other regions, such as refrigerated vehicles, electric vans, or high-capacity long-haul trucks. Fortunately, truck type is treated as an 
exogenous parameter in the model, which means that it can be updated or expanded with minimal computational overhead. Third, 
although the model allows partial consolidation for NCS deliveries, it does not yet support multi-shipment consolidation for CS.
While the current model assumes that each shipment can be accommodated by a single truck, future implementations can adopt 
preprocessing techniques — such as dividing oversized loads into sub-requests — to improve feasibility and generalization in more 
complex delivery scenarios. Future research should aim to expand the geographical scope of the model, integrate a wider variety 
of vehicle types and cargo categories, and incorporate advanced features such as predictive analytics and artificial intelligence for 
dynamic decision-making. In addition, broader consolidation strategies and more complex routing constraints should be explored to 
further enhance both economic and environmental performance. Finally, continuously improving feedback mechanisms for platform 
users will help stakeholders optimize their operations and support real-time decision-making in dynamic freight environments.
